Standard D1.2 — the keystone

These two look identical.
They are not.

<div id="header"> ... </div>

<header> ... </header>
The first is a box you named.
The second means header — to a screen reader, to Google, and to the next person who opens your file.

Who is actually reading your HTML?

ReaderWhat it does with <div id="nav">
A person with eyesNothing — they see the links and use them
A screen readerNothing. It cannot offer "skip to navigation" because it does not know this is navigation
GoogleGuesses. Sometimes wrongly
The next developerReads your id and hopes you meant it

Semantic tags are how you tell the ones that cannot see.

The seven you need

Your page, named properly

ElementHolds
<header>The site title and the nav. Top of the page
<nav>Navigation links. Only navigation
<main>The page's actual content. Exactly one per page
<section>A themed chunk with a heading
<article>Something that would still make sense on its own
<aside>Related but not essential — a sidebar
<footer>Credits, attributions, contact
Build · 55 minutes — every page

Wrap what you already have

<body>
  <header>
    <h1>My Site About Indiana State Parks</h1>
    <nav>
      <ul> ...your existing menu... </ul>
    </nav>
  </header>

  <main>
    ...everything that was already here...
  </main>

  <footer>
    <p>Photo credits and attributions</p>
  </footer>
</body>

You are not writing new content. You are wrapping what is there.

Three rules people break

The ones to get right

  1. One <main> per page. Exactly one. Not zero, not two.
  2. <nav> is for navigation. Not every list of links — the credits list at the bottom is not a nav.
  3. <main> excludes the header and footer. Those repeat on every page; <main> is what makes this page this page.
